Hi, I'm Max. I'm a STAR student at SAP, which means my degree and my job are the same three years: half the time on coursework in business information systems, half on problems that already have people waiting on the answer.

What holds my attention is the seam between a technical question and a commercial one. Whether an optimisation approach is fast enough is a research question. Whether it is fast enough to be worth paying for is a different question, and usually the harder of the two. My thesis sits exactly there — benchmarking quantum optimisation against classical solvers on supply chain problems, looking for the point where the hardware stops being a science project.

That seam is also why the four things I care about are the same thing from different angles. Mathematics is where the guarantee comes from. Data science and AI are where it meets messy input and stops being clean. Markets are the hardest test any model gets, because they are adversarial and they do not care how elegant the derivation was. And research is what you do when nobody has written the answer down yet — which, in practice, is most of the time.

Concretely that has looked like multi-agent decision-making under Bayesian game theory in Walldorf, and deal screening for a private equity team in New York. Different vocabularies, same underlying question: decide well with incomplete information, then be honest about how much of the result was the model and how much was luck.

Outside all of it I wrestled competitively for over ten years and still run long distances. Both taught me more about finishing things than any productivity system has.

The one that is running right now is the machine serving this page: a self-hosted system that handles my own admin. A ticket queue, agents that pick work off it, a messaging bridge so I can task it from my phone. Deliberately over-engineered — segmented networks, a single source of truth for every service, and a written record of every architectural decision including the ones that turned out badly.

The interesting part has not been the agents. It has been everything around them: least privilege for something that executes code it wrote itself, and monitoring that catches silent failure rather than loud failure. Loud failures take care of themselves.
